How does temperature sensation evolve in polar animals?

I study how TRP ion channels evolve to detect temperature in ectotherms, particularly Arctic and Antarctic fishes, and what that means for animals living in the fastest-changing thermal environments on Earth.

Research

How does thermosensation evolve? As thermal environments shift, how do sensory systems adapt? I investigate TRP channels, a family of ion channels that act as molecular thermosensors by turning temperature into electrical signals. I combine transcriptomics, genomics, bioinformatics, electrophysiology, fieldwork, and behavior to answer this across levels of organization, in both vertebrates and invertebrates.

Arctic codfishes

Some areas of the Arctic Ocean are warming at up to seven times the global average, endangering key subsistence and commercial fisheries. I study the evolution of TRP channel sequences, expression, and structures in thermosensory tissues across Arctic codfishes that differ in their preferred thermal environments.

Funded by an NSF Office of Polar Programs postdoctoral research fellowship.

Antarctic notothenioid fishes

Notothenioids make up most of the fish fauna around Antarctica, where water has stayed between -1.9 and +4 °C for millions of years. I am investigating how TRP channels evolved to sense these temperatures, and comparing species that have secondarily adapted to temperate environments.

Funded by the Fulbright Commission; work at the Universidad Austral de Chile and in the Chilean Antarctic.

Texas leaf-cutter ants

Atta texana is the most northerly leaf-cutter ant. Its range is limited not by the ants’ own tolerance, but by the cold sensitivity of the fungus they farm. What is the molecular basis of the ants’ sensitivity to the fungus’s narrow optimal temperature range, and how has it changed as they expanded north?

York et al. 2024. Hymenopteran-specific TRPA channel from the Texas leaf cutter ant (Atta texana) is heat and cold activated and expression correlates with environmental temperature. Insect Science.

About

I am a postdoctoral fellow in the Department of Evolution, Ecology, and Behavior at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, in the lab of Dr. Chris Cheng. I received my PhD in 2022 from the University of Texas at Austin (Dr. Harold Zakon), and my MSc in 2016 from the University of British Columbia (Drs. Bill Milsom and Doug Altshuler), where I was also an undergraduate.

I grew up in Fairbanks, Alaska, where I helped my dad trap arctic ground squirrels and catch wood frogs. Now I live in Urbana, Illinois, with my husband, twins, and a good dog named Pepper. I use she/her pronouns.
